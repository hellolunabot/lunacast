The Lone Gunmen at 25: The Show That Predicted 9/11

# The Lone Gunmen at 25 — Script
📄 SRT 💬 VTT 📜 Script

Transcript

In March of 2001. a television show aired its first episode. It was about a shadowy faction within the government that hacked into a commercial airliner's flight computer and steered it into the World Trade Center. Six months later. the real thing happened. And the creators of The Lone Gunmen said the very first thought in their mind was: did they watch our show? Did they get this idea from us?
I'm Eleanor Sterling. and this is a story about the show that time forgot — the one that was too far ahead of its time. too unlucky in its timing. and yet somehow. exactly on time for us now.
The Lone Gunmen was a spinoff of The X-Files. But they weren't special agents. They were conspiracy theorists. Three of them. to be precise.
John Fitzgerald Byers — the most proper of the trio. an uptight former FCC public relations worker who knew medicine. genetics. and chemistry. and was named after President Kennedy. Melvin Frohike — the oldest. an aging sixties radical and skilled computer hacker. who was revealed to be a former tango champion. And Richard Langly — young. confrontational. a Ramones fan who once brought a tech cocktail party to a standstill by accusing a company's new microchip of secretly collecting data on its users.
The trio's name came from the X-Files episode where Agent X told them about the JFK assassination: "I heard that it was a lone gunman." Byers turned to him and said: "That's why we like you. Mulder. Your ideas are weirder than ours." Twenty-five years later. we would say Langly was prophetic.
These three men had no day jobs. They lived off financial backers and the subscriptions to their underground newsletter. They shared a loft apartment. drove a Volkswagen van from the seventies as their mobile command center. and spent their lives trying to expose the truth. In the X-Files. they started as comic relief. On their own show. they were supposed to be the heroes.
Then the perfect storm arrived. Fox moved the show from Sunday nights — where it was performing well — to Friday night. The death slot. Viewership cratered. Vince Gilligan. one of the co-creators. put it simply: "Timing is another word for luck. If Lone Gunmen had been a year earlier. it could have been a big hit."
Then came September 11th — and with it. a mood that killed the show entirely.
The X-Files itself was two or three seasons past its peak. The show was canceled in June of 2001 — even though its pilot had drawn thirteen million viewers.
Fox didn't want to go gently. The writers wanted to give the characters a proper sendoff — the Gunmen sacrifice themselves to contain a deadly virus. Fox tried to stop them. They told Fox: "We're doing it. You'll have nothing to broadcast if we force their hand." Spotnitz later reflected: "I do regret that that episode didn't end with a laugh — it just ends with sadness."
And yet. Twenty-five years later. Gilligan says something I find rather beautiful: "We need The Lone Gunmen more than ever. Three guys who. trying to save democracy. save the rule of law. I want to think they're still out there somewhere."
The microchip that secretly collects your data? That plot point from the pilot? The tech companies are still doing it. More blatantly. The Gunmen's question — asked at a cocktail party in 2001 — is the question we all should be asking right now.
The show was never put on streaming. You have to buy the DVDs. Gilligan himself encourages it. A ghost show. living in physical form. asking to be found.
If you've never seen The Lone Gunmen — or you care about the intersection of technology. power. and the people who stand against them — there is no better time than now. Thirteen episodes. Six months of television that saw the future coming. Go find them. And give a thought. the next time something unsettling feels too familiar. to three men in a VW van who never stopped trying.
