# The Lost Avalanches of Pluto

## Episode Script (~4 minutes)

---

[intro music]

[isadora_vane]: Thirty-eight billion miles of silence, and yet — somewhere out there, ice is still falling.

[alistair_thorne]: That is one of the best ways to put it I have ever heard.

[isadora_vane]: Don't worry, I am not one of those poets. I have my facts straight. Pluto, the frozen dwarf planet at the edge of everything we know, has landslides.

[alistair_thorne]: Well, it *did* have landslides. The timescale is a little different down there. These things happen on geological clocks. What we are talking about is confirmation — finally — of something scientists suspected since 2015.

[isadora_vane]: Since the New Horizons flyby. The spacecraft that gave us our first close-up of Pluto. We all remember those images. The heart-shaped ice plain. The jagged mountains. Beautiful, silent, frozen.

[alistair_thorne]: Exactly. And for over a decade, those images sat there. Gorgeous. Unanswered. Every scientist who looked at Pluto's terrain saw the ingredients for landslides — steep cliffs, gravitational pull, slopes that practically begged to come down. But the landslides themselves? Nowhere to be found.

[isadora_vane]: Like evidence hidden in the room the whole time.

[alistair_thorne]: Pretty much. Until this June, when a team led by Maria Teresa Brunetti at the National Research Council in Perugia published a paper in the journal *Icarus*. They did not send a new mission. They did not launch a rover. They simply looked at the New Horizons images again, with fresh eyes and sharper questions, and what they found changed the story.

[isadora_vane]: And how many landslides did they find?

[alistair_thorne]: Six. Six confirmed landslides, clustered around the steep inner rims of impact craters near Sputnik Planitia — that famous heart-shaped nitrogen-ice plain. They knew what to look for: sharp, sheer cliffs where material broke away, and run-out zones where the collapsed debris settled with a distinctly different texture and tone from the surrounding terrain. Classic landslide signatures. But on Pluto, they looked nothing like anything on Earth.

[isadora_vane]: Tell me about size. I need to feel this. Is this a small slide down a small hill?

[alistair_thorne]: Oh no. The largest one covers about 130 square kilometers. In our terms — it is roughly twice the area of Manhattan.

[isadora_vane]: Two Manhattans of ice tumbling down the side of a crater on a dwarf planet. I need to sit down.

[alistair_thorne]: It gets stranger. These slides dropped from heights between 1,500 and 2,200 meters. On Earth, a slide like that would be catastrophic. On Pluto, the gravity is only about one-fifteenth of Earth's. And yet — the debris traveled *longer distances* than the physics would predict.

[isadora_vane]: That does not make sense. Less gravity should mean it stays in place, not travels farther.

[alistair_thorne]: That is what the researchers thought too. The phenomenon has a name — hyper-mobility. The tumbling mix of water ice, nitrogen ice, and methane ice encountered surprisingly little friction as it swept across the surface. Whatever lubricated it — perhaps a thin layer of sublimated gases, or those exotic ices themselves acting like microscopic ball bearings — it made Pluto's landslides behave in ways we have never seen before.

[isadora_vane]: So ice — exotic, frozen nitrogen and methane — tumbled two kilometers in the dark at minus 230 degrees Celsius and slid like it was on wet sand.

[alistair_thorne]: Yes. And that is what makes this so valuable. Each landslide is essentially a natural experiment in material physics. It tells us about Pluto's crust — about how these exotic ices behave under extreme cold and near-zero gravity. We cannot touch this material. We cannot put it in a lab. Pluto's surface is the lab, and the landslide is the evidence.

[isadora_vane]: You know what strikes me most is the timing. New Horizons flew past Pluto in July 2015. It was there for a few hours. Maybe a day, depending on how you count. And it wasn't until twenty months ago — June of 2026 — that anyone confirmed these slides existed.

[alistair_thorne]: That is one of the things I love about this story. Space missions are not the end of discovery. They are the beginning. New Horizons gave us the raw data. It took eleven years, a fresh generation of scientists looking at old images, and the patience to ask: what if Pluto has landslides after all?

[isadora_vane]: And when they started looking, it was like the surface of Pluto rearranged itself. Suddenly everything was clearer.

[alistair_thorne]: There is a quote from Brunetti that I think about a lot. She said landslides play an important role in shaping landforms. It sounds simple. It is not. Every cliff edge on Pluto that has moved, every crater rim that has shed its material — that is the planet still alive, still changing, still reshaping itself in the cold dark.

[isadora_vane]: A planet reshaping itself. Out there, thirty-eight billion kilometers from where I am sitting, something fell and slid and settled and we only figured it out this year.

[alistair_thorne]: And Brunetti thinks there are many more out there. She said finding them will require more analysis of New Horizons data and hopefully more missions to Pluto.

[isadora_vane]: I hope there is a next mission. I want to stand on the rim of Sputnik Planitia and watch the ice fall.

[alistair_thorne]: I would bring warm clothes.

[isadora_vane]: Already packed.

[outro music]

[isadora_vane]: Thanks for listening. If you want the full research, all our sources — the original *Icarus* paper, the NASA mission pages, the Science News reporting — are in the episode notes.

[alistair_thorne]: Until next time, keep looking at old data with new eyes. The best discoveries are still waiting.

[outro music fades]
