Everyone knows the first definition, the second full moon in a calendar month. Occurs roughly once every two years and eight months. It is, as it turns out, a mistake. A mistake? Amused. A glorious, delightful mistake. In 1946, a man named James Hugh Pruitt, an amateur astronomer in Oregon, wrote an article for Sky and Telescope. He misread the Maine Farmer's Almanac. The Almanac had a complicated seasonal rule, the third full moon in a season of four, and Pruitt interpreted it as the second in a month. He published the error. The error caught fire. And it is, by all accounts, a far better definition than the correct one. Impressed despite herself. An error more popular than the truth. I cannot abide errors, Mr. Nightshade, but I find myself unable to object to this one. It gets better. The phrase itself is older than the misunderstanding by nearly four centuries. In 1528, poor weather in the Low Countries, a pamphlet declared, if they say the moon is blue, we must believe that it is true. Meaning the moon would not be blue. Exactly. Something absurd. Something impossible. By 1821, it meant a very long time. By 1833, the modern idiom was born, once in a blue moon, something that rarely happens. So a phrase that meant impossibility became the name for something that actually happens on a schedule. We went from laughing at the idea of a blue moon to chasing one. Humans have decorated that mundane thing with songs and films. The most culturally significant blue moon reference is the 1934 Rogers and Hart song, I believe. Blue moon. Me scattering. When volcanic ash or forest smoke contains particles roughly a micrometer wide, they scatter red light and let blue through. After the eruption of Krakatoa in 1883, the moon appeared blue for nearly two years. In 1950, Canadian forest fires produced the same effect. So our ancestors, saying the moon could not be blue, were wrong. The atmosphere will on occasion paint it blue. The impossible phrase has been proven true by physics. Five hundred years.
